How to Write a Performance Review with Claude

You'll finish with a fair, structured performance appraisal you're confident sending — even if your notes are a mess, you have no template, and you've got 20 minutes before the deadline. Paste your raw input, run one prompt, refine twice. That's the whole thing.

TL;DR

  • You'll have a complete draft in under 60 seconds — even from bullet-point notes.
  • Free plan works fine; one conversation is enough for a full review.
  • Claude writes, you fact-check and sign off — the judgment stays yours.
  • What used to take two hours takes 20 minutes, including your edits.

Before you start

  • A Claude.ai account — free plan works fine here; upgrade to Pro if you're running several reviews back to back.
  • Whatever notes you actually have: 1-on-1 scribbles, a Slack thread, project outcomes, a self-assessment form — even messy bullet points work. Don't clean them up first; paste them raw.
  • The review format your client or company expects. If you don't know it off the top of your head, spend 30 seconds checking before you open Claude — this single detail shapes the whole output.
  • The employee's job title and the review period. Two fields. Without them, Claude defaults to generic phrasing that you'll spend time deleting.

How to do it — step by step

1

Collect your raw notes and evidence

Start by opening a blank doc and dumping everything in: 1-on-1 notes, project outcomes, peer feedback, self-assessment answers, any numbers you tracked. Don't edit — paste it all raw. The reason this matters: Claude can only find evidence that's actually there. Spend an extra two minutes retrieving one more specific example — a project name, a metric, a piece of feedback — and that usually saves three rounds of follow-up edits later. If you're pulling from an HR workflow system you've kept through the year, this step takes 90 seconds instead of ten minutes.

2

Define the review format and tone you need

Before moving on, lock in two things: the section headings your client or company expects (Overall Summary, Key Achievements, Areas for Development, Goals for Next Period — whatever their template says), and the tone (formal and evaluative, or coaching-focused and direct). You're doing this now, not inside Claude, because if you skip it you'll get a well-written review in the wrong structure and spend more time reformatting than you saved. If you work with multiple clients who each have their own format, the HR Prompt Builder is worth bookmarking — it's built for exactly this kind of reuse.

3

Run the prompt and get a first draft

Now open Claude and paste the prompt below — fill in the green placeholders, then paste your notes at the bottom. Hit send. This is where Claude usually shines: it reads across all your notes at once, pulls out the recurring themes, and returns a structured draft in professional language in under a minute. Don't try to perfect the prompt before sending — a rough first pass gives you something to react to, which is always faster than writing from scratch. The goal here is a working skeleton, not a final document.

4

Refine tone, evidence, and balance

At this point, read the draft once through and ask yourself two things: does every claim trace back to a real example in your notes, and does the balance between positive and constructive feedback actually match how this person performed? If the output feels generic — phrases like "consistently demonstrates strong communication skills" with nothing behind them — that's your cue to push back in the same conversation. Be specific: "The development section is vague — rewrite it using only the feedback from the design team in my notes" gets you a better result than "make it more specific." The AI for HR Teams hub has more examples of how to phrase these follow-up requests if you get stuck.

5

Final check and personalise before sharing

Now check every factual claim against your original notes — not because Claude is unreliable, but because it occasionally blends two similar examples or slightly misattributes a detail, and you're the one signing this off. Replace any leftover generic phrases with the employee's actual name, real project names, and specific dates. Don't worry if this takes five minutes; it's the step that makes the review feel written by a person who actually knows the employee, not generated from a template. Then paste into your HR platform or document. Start to finish, you should be done in 20 minutes.

The prompt

You are helping me write a professional performance review for one of my employees or team members.

Context:
- Employee name and job title: [e.g. Sarah Chen, Senior Marketing Coordinator]
- Review period: [e.g. January–December 2024]
- Review format: [e.g. Overall Performance Summary / Key Achievements / Areas for Development / Goals for Next Period]
- Desired tone: [e.g. professional and coaching-focused, not punitive]
- Rating scale (if applicable): [e.g. Exceeds / Meets / Below expectations, or leave blank if no rating]

Raw notes and evidence:
[Paste your notes here — bullet points are fine. Include specific examples, project outcomes, feedback received, metrics, and anything relevant to this employee's performance during the review period.]

Using only the evidence in my notes above, write a structured performance review for this employee. Follow the format I specified. For each section, include specific examples drawn from my notes rather than generic statements. Balance positive observations with honest, constructive feedback. Keep the language professional, clear, and direct — avoid jargon and filler phrases like "goes above and beyond."

Do not invent details that are not in my notes. If a section of the format has no supporting evidence in my notes, say so briefly rather than padding with generic content.

Please produce a draft performance review that I can refine and personalise before sharing with the employee.

Example output

Sarah delivered a strong year in the Marketing Coordinator role, taking clear ownership of the Q3 product launch campaign and demonstrating consistent reliability across recurring responsibilities. The campaign generated 2,400 qualified leads in its first month — 20% above the target set in January. Additionally, Sarah redesigned the internal content calendar process, reducing missed deadlines across the team by approximately 30% in H2.

Sarah's written communication is strong in internal documents but less consistent in external-facing copy, where tone can occasionally drift from the brand voice. A structured review step with the brand team before publishing would address this. She has also flagged a desire to build more confidence in data analysis; enrolling in the Q1 analytics workshop would be a practical next step.

How to customise this workflow

For small business owners with no HR background

You'll get a more usable first draft if you add one line to the prompt: "Assume I have no HR background — use plain language and avoid jargon." Then, before pasting your notes, ask Claude in a separate message: "Suggest a simple review format for a 10-person company with no formal appraisal process." It'll give you a structure in 20 seconds that you'd otherwise spend 30 minutes trying to build from a blank doc. The AI for HR Teams hub has more guides written specifically for non-specialists doing HR work on their own.

For independent HR consultants working with multiple clients

If you're doing this for three clients a month, don't rewrite the prompt each time — save a version per client format in your notes tool and just swap in their specific sections, rating scale, and competency framework before pasting the employee notes. That drops the setup from five minutes to under one. The HR Prompt Builder is built for exactly this: you configure the prompt once per scenario and reuse it without starting from scratch.

For 360-degree or peer-inclusive reviews

Don't worry if your input is a mix of sources — paste it all in and label each block clearly: "Peer feedback from design team:", "Self-assessment:", "My observations:". Claude handles multiple sources well and will synthesise across them. More usefully: tell it to flag any significant gaps between how the employee sees themselves and how peers described them. That discrepancy is often the most important thing to bring up in the actual review conversation.

For remote or distributed teams

Remote reviews tend to be thinner because you don't have the informal observation data you'd pick up in an office. Before moving on to the prompt, pull in whatever written trail exists: Slack threads where the person communicated well (or didn't), async retro notes, written project updates. Then add this instruction to your prompt: "Pay specific attention to collaboration quality and async communication — these matter most for remote roles." Otherwise Claude defaults to achievements-and-goals structure and misses what actually matters for distributed work.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use this workflow with the free version of Claude?
Yes — the free plan at claude.ai handles one full review without issue. Where it gets tight is batching: if you're doing five reviews back to back in one session you'll likely hit the message limit. In that case, either spread them across two sessions or move to Claude Pro. For a single review, free is fine.
Is it safe to paste employee information into Claude?
Anthropic doesn't use your conversations to train Claude by default. That said, check your organisation's data handling policy before pasting identifiable employee details into any external tool — some companies have blanket rules about this. The practical workaround: replace the employee's full name with their role label ("the senior coordinator") and use initials for anyone mentioned in the notes. The draft quality is identical, and you stay on the right side of most data policies.
What if I don't have detailed notes — can Claude still help?
Honestly, not much. The prompt explicitly tells Claude not to invent details, so if your notes are thin, the draft will be thin too — and you'll spend your 20 minutes writing, not editing. If that's where you are right now, use this review as the wake-up call to start a simple tracking habit: one bullet per week per direct report is enough. The HR Workflows hub has lightweight systems for doing exactly that, so the next review takes minutes instead of excavation.
How do I make sure the review doesn't sound AI-generated?
Two moves. First, replace every generic phrase in the draft with something specific from your notes — an actual project name, a real date, a number. "Consistently delivers high-quality work" becomes "Delivered the March client audit two days early with zero revisions required." Second, in the same Claude conversation, ask: "Rewrite any filler phrases to be more direct and specific." After one or two passes of that, the output reads like something a thoughtful manager wrote, not a template that got slightly personalised.
