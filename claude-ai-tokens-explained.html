Section 01

Claude does not read words. It reads tokens.

When you send a message to Claude, it does not process your text the way a human reads it — word by word, sentence by sentence. Instead, Claude breaks everything down into tokens: small chunks of text that typically represent about three-quarters of a word.

The word strategy might be one token. The phrase market analysis might be three. Every prompt you write, every document you upload, every reply Claude generates — all of it is measured and counted in tokens.

Why does this matter for you? Your Claude subscription comes with a daily usage limit measured in tokens. Heavy tasks — long documents, complex briefs, extended conversations — consume that budget faster than you might expect. Understanding tokens is the first step to working smarter.

A 10-page Word document, a full email thread, and a detailed system prompt can each consume thousands of tokens before you have typed a single question.

Every Claude conversation draws from a shared token budget. That budget has three distinct sources — and all three are within your power to influence.

Your control level: High

Everything you type, paste, or upload counts towards your token budget. This includes your question, any documents you share, and the full history of the conversation so far. In a long working session — multiple exchanges, several document pastes, iterative rewrites — your input tokens can easily dominate the budget.

Tip: Be specific upfront. One precise prompt uses far fewer tokens than three rounds of vague back-and-forth.

Your control level: Medium

System context is the behind-the-scenes layer: project instructions you have saved, custom personas, tool configurations, and any memory Claude has built from prior sessions. It loads automatically and occupies real token space every single time.

Tip: Leaner project instructions mean more room for the actual work. Audit your project memory every few weeks.

Your control level: Medium

Every word Claude generates consumes tokens too. Open-ended prompts with no format guidance tend to produce longer, more expansive responses. Asking Claude to explain everything will cost noticeably more than asking for three bullet points.

Tip: Specify format and length in your prompt. "In no more than five bullet points" is a simple instruction that pays off consistently.

The compounding effect: in a single working session — reviewing a board report, requesting three rewrites, and asking follow-up questions — all three sources stack together and can quietly consume a large slice of your daily budget.

Most professionals hit their daily token limit not from one large task, but from a series of small inefficiencies that compound across the day.

Five quick wins you can apply today
  • Paste only the relevant section of a document, not the whole file.
  • Specify your desired output format upfront: respond in five bullet points, one sentence each.
  • Start a fresh conversation for each distinct task rather than continuing long threads.
  • Review and trim your project memory every few weeks — remove outdated instructions.
  • Ask one clear question per message. Compound questions produce compound and expensive answers.
